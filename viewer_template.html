How many batteries can the arbitrage market take?

Model of day-ahead arbitrage as more batteries enter. The fleet charges and discharges in the day's cheapest and most expensive hours, and that moves the price itself. At what fleet size does revenue per MW fall, and how much does the spread shrink? It depends on how sensitive the price is. That is estimated from historical load, wind, solar and price (see further down), or you can set it yourself.12, 13

Empirical basis: how much does the price move per GW?

The estimate compares the hourly price with residual load (consumption minus wind and solar) over the last 24 months, accounting for month and hour of day. Wind and weather make residual load vary on its own, and the price follows. That is the same thing a battery does when it charges or discharges.14, 15

Definitions and notes

Day-ahead price
The power exchange's price for the next day, set the day before, per bidding zone and in euro per megawatt-hour (EUR/MWh). It is not the end-customer price, and it includes neither taxes, grid fees nor the retailer's margin. Intraday and balancing prices are not included.
Bidding zone
Sweden is divided into four (SE1–SE4). Finland, Denmark (DK1 and DK2) and Germany (here DE-LU, together with Luxembourg) are shown for comparison.
Arbitrage depth
How large a battery fleet the day-ahead market can absorb before arbitrage stops paying. The saturation point is the fleet size at which revenue per MW has fallen to a given share of the first MW's revenue.
Price impact
How many euro per megawatt-hour the price moves when a 1 GW fleet charges (price rises) or discharges (price falls) at full power. It is the model's most important and most uncertain assumption.
  1. The arbitrage depth model. The fleet consists of identical batteries with power N and duration 1, 2 or 4 hours, 88 % round-trip efficiency, and charges only from the grid. The batteries are price takers with perfect foresight of the day's day-ahead prices, and are empty at the end of the day. The price in that hour is shifted linearly with the fleet's net flow (price impact in euro per MWh and GW). Because the batteries push the price up when they charge and down when they discharge, revenue per MW falls as the fleet grows. The calculation is based on hourly prices for every fourth day over the last 12 months, scaled up to a year.
  2. Limitations. The result depends entirely on the price impact. The model has a single price level per zone and does not account for trade with neighbouring zones that dilutes the effect, for supply and demand changing over time, or for batteries also selling in intraday and ancillary service markets. It does not include grid fees, operations, degradation or investment costs. It shows an upper limit for what day-ahead arbitrage yields, and not what an individual installation will earn.
  3. How price impact is estimated. For each zone a regression of hourly price on residual load (consumption minus wind and solar, in GW) is run over the last 24 months, with fixed effects for month (which captures fuel prices and hydrology) and for hour of day split into weekdays and weekends. Prices are capped at the 1st–99th percentile so that single spikes do not dominate. Standard errors are cluster-robust by week. Because weather co-varies between zones and the zones are priced together, four variants are run: own residual load only, with Germany as a control, with all other zones as controls (the main estimate, including the five Norwegian zones), and the same on only the latest 12 months. The bands in the charts span the three controlled variants, and the variant without controls is shown only for comparison because it comes out too high.
  4. What the estimate does not show. It measures how the price has moved given the variations in residual load that have actually occurred, often a few hundred MW up to about a GW in a Swedish zone. A battery fleet of several GW lies outside that range, and the line is then an extrapolation. When the batteries fill up the interconnectors to neighbouring zones, the zones decouple and the price becomes more sensitive, so real saturation may come faster than a straight line suggests. The relationship is also affected by things that are not included, for example flows, nuclear outages and hydrology.
